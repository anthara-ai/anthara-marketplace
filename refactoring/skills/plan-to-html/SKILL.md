# Refactoring plan as a pitch

Write `docs/refactoring/<module-slug>-plan.html`: a page that argues for the refactoring, aimed at the people whose agreement the developer needs.

The markdown plan is the document a developer works from. This page is the document a team says yes from. They carry the same numbers and they are not the same document. The markdown is organised for someone doing the work in order. The page is organised to win an argument in a room where nobody has to agree.

The page changes no code and adds no analysis. Every number on it comes from the markdown plan.

## What this page is for

Someone read a 4,000-line class, spent an afternoon on the forensics, and now has to convince a lead who is protective of the roadmap and two seniors who have watched a refactoring go wrong before. Nobody in that room is obliged to read anything. The page has to earn the next five minutes in the first thirty seconds.

That has consequences for how it is built:

**Lead with the argument, not the method.** A reader who scrolls no further should already know why this module, why now, and what it costs. How the numbers were gathered matters to the one person who asks, and that person can scroll.

**One number does the work.** Pick the figure that makes the case for this particular module and give it the top of the page. When the plan's headline measure is itself a pair, such as the files and the functions a routine change costs today, lead with the pair and not with one half of it. It is usually about pain the room has already felt, such as how much the file grew this year, how many commits landed in it, or how many files a routine change touches. Dependency counts and line counts are true and rarely move anyone.

**Answer the three objections before they are raised.** Why now rather than later, what breaks, and what it costs. A plan that cannot answer all three is not ready to be pitched, and the page should say which one it cannot answer rather than route around it.

**Evidence sits behind the claim it supports.** Tables, methodology and the full step detail belong in the appendix, reachable by the person who asks for them and out of the way of the person who does not.

The failure to avoid is a faithful rendering of the markdown with charts added. Section-for-section transcription produces a report, and a report is read by people who already agreed.

## Two halves, one file

The page is a deck with an appendix behind it, in one self-contained file.

**The slides carry the argument.** Each slide is a 1920 by 1080 canvas, scaled to the window on screen and printed one per page, so the same file projects in the meeting and attaches to the pull request. A slide makes one claim. The heading is the claim, the lede grounds it in a number from the plan, and the exhibit shows that number. Whatever the claim does not need is not on the slide. An idea that carries weight but does not earn a slide goes on the slide it belongs to as a one-line footnote, with no new number and no new exhibit. An idea that cannot earn a footnote does not ship.

**The appendix carries the audit.** After the last slide, a scrolling section that reflows at any width holds the plan's evidence as table cards: every step in full, the hotspot table, the change coupling, the trace of the problem statement's path through the code, the contract, the safety net, and the measures. Each card names the plan section its rows come from in its header and closes with a caveat row that says what the table cannot show. Every file, function and commit in it links to the repository host at the plan's commit hash, so a skeptic opens the thing being claimed rather than taking the page's word. The slides are for the room. The appendix is for the person who says "show me".

The look, the mechanics and the components of both halves come from `references/deck-scaffold.html`. Copy it, keep its head, delete its catalogue slides, and compose from its components. The scaffold fixes the design system, so that every pitch this skill writes looks like the same firm wrote it, and it fixes the page mechanics: scaling, keyboard navigation, print, narrow viewports, and a complete page with JavaScript off. It fixes no slide list. Which slides exist, in what order, and with which chart is decided in step 5 for this plan and this audience. Adding a colour, a font or a component the scaffold does not have is a design decision that needs a reason the developer would accept. The scaffold's header comment documents every component, the colour meanings, the chart rules and the link forms, and it is the reference for filling.

## Order of work

The skill runs five phases in one session: get the plan, name the audience, argue, render, check. The argument is written down before any markup exists, as an outline the developer can redirect. The check phase is four mechanical gates, one cold read by an agent playing the audience, and one browser pass, and the hand-over waits for all of them. A second audience for the same plan re-enters at the argue phase, never at the plan.


1. **The voice and the theme.** Invoke `refactoring:incubyte-writing-voice` and write every sentence on the page in that voice, since the page is read outside the team that wrote it. It governs how sentences are built. Where it and anything here disagree on that, it wins. Invoke it once per session.

   The scaffold ships Anthara's theme. Its page background, text and accent greens, warning amber, font stack and the wordmark on the title slide are already in `references/deck-scaffold.html`, so a page for an Anthara repository takes no theming step and no question. Only when the plan is for another company's repository does the theme change: read that brand from the repository first, since a `globals.css`, a Tailwind config, a `design.md` or a logo component is the design system as the team ships it, and ask once for the company's website only when the repository carries none. Either way, fill `--paper`, `--ink`, `--accent`, `--caution`, `--sans` and the brand slot, and nothing else. The accent is the one colour the brand uses for links or buttons, never a warning or error colour, and it must pass contrast against the paper, or the shipped theme stays. Name any font in a stack that ends in the system's own and load nothing, so the page still opens offline.

2. **Get the plan.** Invoke the `refactoring:plan` skill with `$ARGUMENTS` unchanged and let it finish. One exception: when `docs/refactoring/<module-slug>-plan.md` already exists and the commit hash in its summary block equals the current `git rev-parse --short HEAD`, ask in one line whether to render that plan or re-run the analysis, and render it when the developer says nothing specific. A plan written at a different hash is stale, so re-run the analysis rather than rendering it silently.

3. **Ask who the pitch is for.** Ask once, with `AskUserQuestion`, unless the arguments already name an audience such as "for the CTO" or "for my team". Offer the four rooms in `references/audiences.md`: **my team**, the developers who will do the work; **tech lead or EM**, who approves the time; **leadership or product**, non-technical; **a mixed room**, such as sprint planning. Then read that audience's brief in full. It lists the questions the room walks in with, in order, what leads, which exhibits are drawn and which go to the appendix, the vocabulary register, and the decision the closing slide asks for. The brief decides the shape of the page. It never changes the numbers.

4. **Read the plan and pull its numbers.** Every number on the page comes from the markdown. Compute nothing new and invent nothing. Percentages are allowed only when both the numerator and the denominator are in the plan, and the page shows both. When the argument needs a figure the plan does not carry, say so in place rather than estimating, and keep it brief.

   Also collect what the links need: the local clone, the origin remote, and the plan's full 40-character hash from its summary block. The plan's own file mentions are already links, written by `check-links.mjs`, and the page gets its links from the same tool in step 7. Never write a repository link by hand, because a hand-written one carries the short hash and Azure DevOps refuses it. A repository with no remote gets no links and one sentence in the provenance footer saying so.

5. **Argue before you render.** Write the argument outline: one line per slide, each line a claim in the audience's words, the plan figure that proves it, and the exhibit that shows it or "no exhibit". Build it from the brief's questions in the brief's order, not from the plan's sections. Then read it back against the brief. A slide that answers a question the brief does not ask is cut or sent to the appendix, however good its chart would look. A question the brief asks that has no slide is a gap: fill it from the plan, or name it on the page as unanswered. Then apply the load test from the brief to each line: the heading alone answers the slide's question, there is one exhibit, and nothing beside it restates what it shows. Count the numbers a slide will carry, and above about a dozen move the detail to the appendix. Show the outline to the developer in the conversation, one line per slide, and render unless they redirect. A pitch is usually four to six slides between the title and the appendix divider, and never one per markdown section.

   The plan carries the material for every question the briefs ask, and the scaffold has a component for each answer. The felt cost is the plan's trace of the problem statement, drawn as the path component, together with the commits that last made that change. Why now is the hotspot trend, drawn as columns, and the concentration of knowledge, stated as a share. Will it spill is the contract, counted as tiles, beside the safety net. What it costs is the commit count and a schedule derived from the dependency lines, since a plan never carries engineer days. The shape of the change is the before-and-after diagram, which leads for a developer audience and is appendix material for everyone else. The decisions are the plan's questions for the team plus the go or no-go on the first phase, and they close the deck because they are what the meeting exists to produce.

6. **Write the page** to `docs/refactoring/<module-slug>-plan.for-<audience>.html`, where the audience slug is one of `team`, `tech-lead`, `leadership` or `mixed-room`, starting from a copy of `references/deck-scaffold.html` with the theme from step 1. The audience is in the filename because the page is for that audience by definition, and a second pitch from the same plan sits beside the first rather than overwriting it. Keep the title slide and the appendix divider, fill them, and delete every section carrying `data-catalogue`. Replace the scaffold's header comment with four or five lines of provenance, since the fill guidance is for you and not for the reader of the delivered page. Compose the argument slides from the scaffold's components, one `.slide-frame` per slide. Inline CSS, inline SVG and inline script only, no external resource of any kind, so the file opens from disk, attaches to a pull request and pastes into a wiki. A font loaded from a font service is an external resource, so the stack ends in the system's own fonts.

   Generate the shape-of-the-change diagram, never draw it. Write one small JSON spec per panel, with a column and a row for every box and a from and a to for every edge, and run `node <skill>/references/draw-shape.mjs spec.json`. The spec format is in the script's header. The generator places the boxes on a grid, routes every edge as horizontal and vertical segments, gives a box with several edges one port per edge so no two arrowheads land on the same point, draws one arrowhead shape at one size for every edge, and refuses a backwards edge, a label that does not fit, or two boxes on one cell. It exists because an edge routed by hand runs through an unrelated box, arrives at an odd angle, or piles three arrowheads on one point, and none of that is visible when reading the markup. Keep the spec files beside the page under `docs/refactoring/<module-slug>-shape.<panel>.json`, since the verifier sends fixes back to them. A panel is a story, not an import graph: the generator refuses more than eight boxes, more than eight edges, or a box with more than three edges in or out. Draw the edges the slide's claim rests on, collapse every module the claim does not need into one counted group box, and let the panel's title carry the full statement. A change that still does not fit goes to the appendix as a table of edges instead of a diagram. A cycle that exists today is drawn as an edge of kind `back`, allowed in the today panel only, and a dependency that is not an import, an event, a queue, a shared table or a config key, as kind `indirect`, so the reader knows what the arrow stands for. And when no step adds or removes an edge between modules, as with Long Function or dead code inside one class, there is no shape to draw: skip the shape slide and the panels, and let the team deck lead with the X-ray of the hot file. Two identical panels are not a diagram. Spawn the graph verifier from step 7 on the spec files and their SVGs now, before writing a slide, and get **accurate** first, because a graph fix found after the prose exists rewrites that prose and every number that hung on it, and that is what turned one cold read into several.

   Write every heading as one plain claim in the room's words, at most about sixteen words, with no second clause hung on with ", and" or ", which". Whatever the heading does not say goes in the lede. Write every column header as the thing the column holds and its unit, in two to six words a reader outside the team can parse without the plan, such as "Commits in 12 months" or "Deeply nested lines". A one-word term from the method, such as "Net" or "Pair", and a question, such as "In plan?", are both refused by the checker. The method's definition goes in the card's caveat row when the reader needs it.

   Keep every slide inside the 1080 canvas. The slide grows rather than clips, which is a safety valve for unexpected font metrics and not room to spend. A slide that renders taller than 1080 spills onto a second printed page, and it is a sign that the slide carries two claims rather than one. Fix it by moving something to the appendix, never by shrinking type. Every appendix sheet is reachable from a pill link on the divider slide.

   Then build the appendix from the plan's tables. The step card is exhaustive: every T and R step from the markdown, in plan order, with its full what, why, steps, check and commit text behind a native disclosure carrying `id="step-<id>"`, so a step that never made a slide is still one click away. When the plan splits a step into sub-steps, the disclosure lists them as `ol.ax-substeps`, one anchored item per sub-step with its id, a few words on what moves, one sentence of what, and the check it leans on, with a blind chip when it has none, and never as a paragraph that runs the sub-steps together. Every step or sub-step id that appears on a slide, whether as a chip or in a table cell, is a link to its own disclosure or item, and the first slide that uses the ids says once what R and T stand for, because a reader outside the plan meets "R3 · 5" cold. The evidence sheets carry the hotspot table, the change coupling, the problem statement's trace, the contract, the safety net and the measures, as the plan has them. A table that enumerates a full population closes with a totals row. A table that shows the top few of a larger set closes with a row for the whole set, labelled as such, so the sample reads against its base. Each card's source label names the plan section its rows come from, and that name is a link to the heading in the markdown beside the page, by heading slug, so a reader moves from the table to the plan in one click. The provenance sheet states the hash, the window, the commits excluded as noise and why, and what could not be measured, and its footer is four terse bullets, the source and commit, the method, the access the links need, and that nothing in the code changed. The counting rules live once, in the sheet's "how we counted" column, and the footer never repeats them, because a sheet that says the same fact twice reads as padding.

7. **Verify.** Four mechanical gates, then one reader, then a browser pass. Each gate runs until it is silent before the next starts.

   **The page checker** reads the finished page and reports unfilled slots, leftover catalogue slides, the scaffold's fill-guidance comment left in place, em dashes, external resources, bare font sizes in inline styles, dangling internal anchors, links into the plan that name no heading, appendix sheets with no pill link, charts with no accessible name, a heading that joins two claims or runs long, a column header that is one word or a question, a semicolon or a long parenthesis in the prose, a bold lead-in with no space after it, and the diagram: more than eight boxes or eight edges in one panel, boxes that collide, labels that overrun their box, boxes outside the viewBox, an edge that names a box that is not drawn, and any edge that runs through a box that is neither of its endpoints.

   ```bash
   node <skill>/references/check-page.mjs docs/refactoring/<module-slug>-plan.for-<audience>.html
   ```

   **The link tool** wraps every file mention that exists at the plan's commit in a link to the repository host, and verifies every link against the local clone: the path exists at the commit, the hash is the full one, and the line range is inside the file. Run it with `--fix` once and then without, and the second run must be silent.

   ```bash
   node <plan skill>/references/check-links.mjs docs/refactoring/<module-slug>-plan.for-<audience>.html --repo . --hash <full hash> --fix
   node <plan skill>/references/check-links.mjs docs/refactoring/<module-slug>-plan.for-<audience>.html --repo . --hash <full hash>
   ```

   **The number gate** reads the page's text and the markdown plan and reports every number on the page that the plan does not carry. A number the plan lacks was computed or invented while writing slides, and the cold readers found more of those than anything else: a percentage rounded from two counts, a sum of rows, a test count plus the tests a step adds. Fix each one by writing the plan's own figures, or by saying it in words, never by adding the number to the plan.

   ```bash
   node <skill>/references/page-text.mjs docs/refactoring/<module-slug>-plan.for-<audience>.html > /tmp/<module-slug>.<audience>.read.txt
   node <skill>/references/check-numbers.mjs /tmp/<module-slug>.<audience>.read.txt docs/refactoring/<module-slug>-plan.md
   ```

   **The graph verifier** is a separate agent whose only job is to check that the diagram is accurate. Step 6 ran it on the panels before the slides existed, so this run confirms the page carries those panels unchanged. Spawn `refactoring:dependency-graph-verifier` with the page path, the plan path, the clone path and the full hash. It reads every box and edge from the SVG's `data-node`, `data-from` and `data-to` attributes, confirms each edge of the today panel in the code at the commit with a file and line, reports any dependency between drawn boxes that the diagram omits, traces each new box and edge of the after panel to the step that creates it, and checks that every label names something real. It answers **accurate** or **not accurate** with the exact spec changes. Apply them to the spec, regenerate the panel, run the page checker again, and spawn the verifier again. Repeat until it answers accurate. A diagram the verifier has not passed does not ship, and a diagram that cannot be made accurate is replaced by the appendix table of edges.

   **The cold reader** is a separate agent that reads the page once as a member of the room it was written for, with no knowledge of the project, and reports every place it stopped. The mechanical gates cannot see a term used before its gloss, a sentence that reads as praise when it means cost, or two numbers on one slide that do not add up, and every one of those was found by a cold reader and not by the author. Spawn `refactoring:cold-reader` with the reading-order text the number gate used, regenerated if the page changed since, and the audience from step 3:

   ```bash
   node <skill>/references/page-text.mjs docs/refactoring/<module-slug>-plan.for-<audience>.html > /tmp/<module-slug>.<audience>.read.txt
   ```

   The reader answers **ready** or **not ready** with the fixes, one per line, each quoting the page. Treat each fix as a diagnosis, not a prescription. A reader who stopped on a word will suggest a definition, and a definition is the most expensive fix a slide can take. Fix a stop by cutting the term, renaming it to a plain word, or moving the detail to the appendix, in that order, and add a sentence only when nothing else works and the copy budget still holds. The page checker enforces the budgets, so run it after every reader round: the two gates pull against each other on purpose, and a page passes only when both are silent. Spawn one reader per audience the page is for, and a mixed room gets the product reader, since the developers in it can read past what the product manager cannot. The reader reads once. Apply every fix it lists, and apply each one everywhere the same words appear, since a phrase flagged on a heading has a twin in the title, a chip and an appendix cell, so grep the page for the phrase before moving on. A fix is a cut, a rename or a move, never a new sentence, because new prose is the one thing the reader has not read. Then run the four mechanical gates again until they are silent. There is no second reader round. Whatever a fix could not remove goes to the developer as reader notes, one line each. One read is enough when the order above was kept, plan frozen, diagram verified before the slides, numbers checked before the reader, because a second read of the same page mostly finds the text the first round's fixes added.

   Then open the page in a browser if one is available, Claude in Chrome or the equivalent, for the four things only a rendered page can show:

   - **No slide is taller than 1080 at the design width.** The overflow is silent, so measure rather than look: read each `offsetHeight` from `document.querySelectorAll('.slide')`. A slide that grew is carrying two claims, and the fix is to move one to the appendix.
   - **No wrapped text overlaps a mark**, every chart draws to scale, and every diagram is wide enough to read. Divide each `.shape svg` rendered width by the slide's current scale and require at least 750px at the 1920 design width, which is a 13px label at 11px. A diagram that fits the markup can still be illegible on the page when a flex container squeezes it.
   - **Every disclosure opens**, and the appendix reflows at phone width with no horizontal scrollbar on the page itself.
   - **Text stays readable against the themed background**, which matters most when step 1 pulled an accent from a company website.

   Never stall on verification. Tell the developer in one line what the four gates reported, how many rounds the graph verifier took, how many fixes the cold reader listed and how many remain as reader notes, and whether a browser ran.

8. **Then.** Tell the developer both paths, the markdown and the HTML, who the page was written for, and that nothing in the code has changed. Mention that a pitch for a different room is one more render from the same plan, at the same hash, with a different brief. Suggest opening the HTML in the meeting, with the arrow keys stepping through the slides, and the markdown when doing the work.

## Judgement, not a template

How the argument is made is a judgement call for each plan and each audience. A module with one dominant hotspot argues differently from a module with debt spread across a dozen files. A plan with 411 existing tests argues differently from one where every step runs blind. Read what this plan actually says and make the case that fits it.

What holds regardless:

**When the plan has a problem statement, its trace is the lead exhibit.** The list of files a developer touches today, beside the shorter list after the plan, is the most concrete thing the plan produces and the thing the room argues with. Draw it as the path component. A before-and-after bar pair of the same measure beside it says the same thing twice, so use the bar pair only when the plan has no trace to draw.

**The diagram shows the shape of the change, not the dependency graph.** A developer audience reads it before anything else, so it earns its space, but only while it stays readable. The generator caps it at eight boxes and eight edges per panel. Past that, collapse every collaborator the plan does not touch into one box labelled with its count, because an untouched collaborator drawn individually costs a row and returns nothing to the argument. If the collapsed diagram still overflows, do not draw it: put the dependency change in the appendix as a table with one row per edge, which reflows at any size and cannot overlap itself. A plan whose diagram cannot be drawn at all is usually a plan whose steps add up to a rewrite, and the markdown plan is required to say so rather than let the page imply otherwise.

**Charts are for the two or three claims that carry the argument.** Draw the ones that persuade this audience, at a size a room can read from a laptop screen. A chart nobody stops on is worse than a sentence, since it costs space and attention and returns neither.

**Every mark is to scale.** A before bar and an after bar share one maximum. A column chart's tallest column is the largest value and the rest are proportional. A chart with a fixed-width "before" bar across every row is a picture of a ratio drawn to look like a magnitude, and a reader who notices stops trusting the rest of the page.

**A share is labelled as a share and a magnitude as a magnitude.** A bar that draws a percentage carries that percentage as its label. A bar that draws a count carries the count and its unit. Raw counts belong in stat cards, prose and appendix tables, never as the label on a bar whose width is a share.

**Every chart's numbers appear in text beside it.** Someone will ask for the figure. A chart is a summary of a table and never a substitute for one.

**Restraint reads as credible.** The scaffold has three colour meanings and no more. The accent marks what the plan targets and every "after the plan" number. Grey marks what is left alone and every "today" number. The caution tint marks a medium risk, a step that runs without a test, and a gap that blocks a step, so a reader who sees it knows to slow down. A fourth colour makes a reader hunt for a meaning that is not there. Check the themed accent before setting it, since an accent that fails contrast against its own background, or that the site uses for warnings and errors, ships a page that is hard to read or that quietly flags the plan as a problem. Fall back to the default accent rather than either.

**Headings are direct.** The heading says the one thing the slide claims, in words the room uses, and stops. "The main file tripled in four months" is a heading. "The file that carries the flow nearly tripled in four months, and a cleanup was undone within eight weeks" is a lede wearing a heading's type size, and it costs the room a re-read. The same rule holds for column headers: the header says what the column holds and its unit, and never a term the reader has to look up in the plan.

**Gloss the method's words on first use, in the room's words.** Provider, module, path, hotspot, seam, characterisation test, no test and deep lines are the plan's vocabulary, and a cold reader meets each of them one to three slides before its only explanation. Define each the first time it appears on the page, in the same sentence, such as "a hotspot, a file that changes unusually often", and use the short word afterwards. Name what you cite: "libs/integrations", never "a neighbouring library". A before-and-after claim keeps one unit on both sides, so "seven files today, one file after" and never "three commits today, one file after". Every count on a slide adds up to the total on that slide, and the appendix's totals agree with the title's, because a reader who finds one sum wrong stops trusting every other number. Where the plan gives no engineer-days, say so once and say what the plan gives instead, and never bury that sentence in a footnote.

**Bold is a handle, not a sentence.** In the appendix, bold marks the two-to-five-word handle that opens a caveat row and the id of a sub-step, and nothing else. Field labels inside a disclosure are small uppercase labels, not bold words. A page where a third of the appendix text is bold has no emphasis left, and the checker flags any bold span over six words.

**Every sentence is a sentence.** The writing voice asks for complete sentences with a subject and a verb, and the place this slips is the appendix, where a sub-step or a table cell is written as a noun pile: "Empty client, module registration, constructor injection." Write "This step creates an empty client, registers it in the module, and injects it into both services." The checker cannot see a missing verb, so read every appendix paragraph once for this before the hand-over.

**Display copy and running prose have different shapes.** Headings, ledes, footnotes and caveats are connected sentences in the writing voice. Cards, tiles and cells carry one claim per line, three or four lines to a card, with no connectors between the lines. A card slot never holds a paragraph.

**The page loads nothing.** A font, a chart library, an icon. Any of them breaks the moment the page is attached to a pull request or opened offline.

**The page is complete with JavaScript off.** Script scales the canvas and handles the keys. Disclosures are native `<details>`. With script off the slides reflow as a readable single column, which is also what a narrow viewport gets.

**Every chart carries an accessible name.** The page is pasted into wikis and read on other people's machines, some of them with a screen reader.

**Gaps are placed where they are useful, not collected at the end.** A gap that blocks the work, such as a test baseline that was never run green, belongs next to the step it blocks, as a prerequisite in the caution tint. A gap that is merely uncomputed belongs in small text near the chart that would have shown it, and in the provenance sheet's "not measured" column. A closing section listing everything that could not be measured reads as a confession and hands the room its objection.

## Honest persuasion

Persuasion here is selection and order. The page argues by leading with the strongest true thing, never by softening a weak one.

- **No number appears that is not in the plan.** Every figure is traceable to the markdown. Where a gap matters, the page says so.
- **No chart overstates its data.** See the scale and labelling rules above.
- **A ranking says it is a ranking.** When a slide shows the three hottest files of twelve, or the two riskiest steps of eleven, the slide says so in plain words and the appendix has the rest. A reader who assumes the list is exhaustive has been misled.
- **A risk the plan rated medium stays medium on the page.** Naming the riskiest step and what catches it is more convincing than a page where everything is low risk.
- **What the plan will not fix stays visible.** A plan that removes 1,200 lines from a 4,500-line file has not fixed the file, and a reader who works out that omission alone stops believing the rest.
- **Names locate knowledge and never grade people.** The plan's code age and knowledge tables carry author names because the history does. On the page they answer "who do we ask", and nothing else. Prefer the role at every audience level, such as "the author of 84 percent of the commits in this folder", because the role carries the argument and the name does not. Use the name only where the reader has to go and find that person, such as a decision that assigns a reviewer. Whenever a person is named or countable anywhere on the page, the appendix divider carries the one-line fairness note, and the table's caveat row says what the count is for.
- **The page and the markdown never disagree.** They are written from the same run at the same hash. Re-run both rather than editing one.

## Done when

The file exists beside the markdown plan, named for its audience, and opens from disk with no network. It answers that audience's questions in that audience's order, and it reads as a pitch to that audience rather than as the markdown with tags.
